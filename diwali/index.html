Diwali (Deepavali) — Festival of Lights

Light, hope, and new beginnings

Diwali is one of the most widely celebrated festivals in India and across the world. It is known for glowing oil lamps (diyas), colorful rangoli, family gatherings, festive sweets, and the symbolism of light overcoming darkness.

A common greeting is “Happy Diwali!” or “Shubh Diwali”—wishing joy, prosperity, and peace.

What is Diwali?

Diwali (also spelled Deepavali) is a major festival celebrated by Hindus, Jains, Sikhs, and many Buddhists, along with people of other backgrounds who enjoy its spirit of togetherness. The festival typically falls in October or November and is based on the lunar calendar.

The name “Deepavali” comes from Sanskrit words that mean “a row of lamps.” Homes and streets are lit with diyas, candles, and decorative lights to create a warm, welcoming glow.

Meaning and symbolism

While traditions vary by region and community, Diwali is widely seen as a celebration of light over darkness, knowledge over ignorance, and good over evil.

  • Diyas and lights: hope, clarity, and protection.
  • Cleaning and decorating: preparing for a fresh start.
  • Sharing sweets and gifts: gratitude and community.
  • Prayers: seeking well-being, prosperity, and peace.

In many Hindu traditions, Diwali is associated with the return of Lord Rama to Ayodhya, and with Lakshmi Puja—welcoming the goddess Lakshmi, a symbol of prosperity and good fortune. In Jain traditions, it commemorates Mahavira’s attainment of nirvana. Sikhs celebrate Bandi Chhor Divas, marking the release of Guru Hargobind Ji and other prisoners.

The five days of Diwali (common structure)

In many places, Diwali is celebrated over five days. Names and customs can vary, but the flow often looks like this:

  • Dhanteras: cleaning, shopping, and honoring health and prosperity.
  • Naraka Chaturdashi (Chhoti Diwali): early lights, preparations, and family time.
  • Diwali / Lakshmi Puja: prayer, diyas, sweets, and celebrations.
  • Govardhan Puja / Annakut: gratitude, sharing meals, community offerings.
  • Bhai Dooj: celebrating sibling bonds and care.

Traditions you might see

Diwali is both festive and meaningful—some people focus on worship and quiet family rituals, while others emphasize parties, music, and bright decorations.

Rangoli

Colorful floor art made with powders, flowers, or rice—often at the entrance to welcome guests.

Diyas & lanterns

Small oil lamps and paper lanterns symbolize light, warmth, and new beginnings.

New clothes

Many wear festive outfits to mark the occasion and to honor the holiday’s joyful spirit.

Sharing & charity

Visiting friends, exchanging sweets, and helping neighbors are central to the celebration.

Tip: If you’re attending a Diwali celebration for the first time, a thoughtful gesture is to bring a box of sweets, flowers, or a simple card with a warm greeting.

Food and sweets

Food is a big part of Diwali. Families often prepare special dishes and share sweets with friends and neighbors. Favorites differ by region, but common treats include:

  • Ladoo (round sweets made from flour/semolina, ghee, and sugar)
  • Barfi (fudge-like squares, often with nuts)
  • Jalebi (crispy spirals soaked in syrup)
  • Kaju katli (cashew-based sweet with a delicate texture)

Many celebrations also include savory snacks, festive thalis, and meals shared with guests—because generosity is part of the joy.

Celebrate kindly (and safely)

Diwali is at its best when it feels welcoming to everyone—neighbors, pets, and the environment included.

  • Be considerate with noise: set reasonable times and check local rules.
  • Mind air quality: choose lights, diyas, and gatherings over heavy smoke.
  • Use fire safely: keep lamps stable, never leave flames unattended, and keep water nearby.
  • Include others: invite someone new, share a plate, or exchange a kind message.

FAQ

When is Diwali celebrated?

Diwali follows the lunar calendar, so the date changes each year. It usually falls in October or November. Many communities celebrate across several days, with the main night commonly centered on Lakshmi Puja.

What does “Deepavali” mean?

“Deepavali” is often translated as “a row of lamps,” reflecting the tradition of lighting diyas to symbolize hope and guidance.

How can I wish someone a happy Diwali?

“Happy Diwali!” is perfect. You can also say “Shubh Diwali” (auspicious Diwali). If you’re writing a message, a simple “Wishing you light, joy, and peace this Diwali” is always appreciated.

Is Diwali only one night?

Many people think of Diwali as a single evening, but in many traditions it’s a multi-day festival. The exact structure can differ by region, family customs, and community.

Quick facts

  • Also called: Deepavali
  • Theme: light, renewal, gratitude
  • Common symbols: diyas, rangoli, sweets
  • Shared values: generosity, family, community

A simple at-home idea

Make a small rangoli with flowers or colored paper, light a diya (or a safe LED candle), and share a sweet with someone—by visit, message, or delivery.

Small is meaningful: the point is warmth and connection, not perfection.

Pronunciation

Diwali is commonly pronounced “dee-WAH-lee.” Deepavali is commonly pronounced “dee-PAH-vuh-lee.”

If you’re unsure, it’s always okay to ask—most people appreciate the effort.
